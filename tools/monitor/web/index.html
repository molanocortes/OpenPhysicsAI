    How these numbers are measured

    Every two seconds a recorder on each node reads its processor's own counters: floating-point operations retired (a vector instruction counted with the operations it does, a fused multiply-add as two), instructions, cycles, and how busy each thread was, in the node's Linux, where the jobs run. Checked on the desktop against a loop with a known count: 2.0010 × 109 counted, 2.0010 × 109 expected. Each machine keeps a line a minute, so nights you sleep through are here when you come back.

    The MacBook is read on this Mac: each core from the Mach call host_processor_info, memory from host_statistics64, pressure and swap from sysctl, the time since your last input from CoreGraphics, power from pmset. macOS gives ordinary programs no floating-point counters, so its operations (≈, hatched) are an estimate that falls short on purpose: 0.2 per instruction of the lab's own programs, each process's instructions read with proc_pid_rusage. The nodes measure 0.78 and 0.41 operations per instruction, and the M2's 128-bit vectors need up to twice the instructions of the nodes' 256-bit ones; everything else is left out. The estimate is never added to the nodes' measured totals.

    Each slice of the history takes the records it overlaps, weighted by the time they share; Peak is the busiest two-second sample (or minute, in older records). Shaded: 22:00 to 07:00.

    Apollo 11: two guidance computers, at least 11.72 µs an instruction (AGC), over 8 d 3 h 18 min 35 s (Apollo 11): at most 1.2 × 1011 instructions, matched one for one with ours. Cray-1: 160 million operations a second at its peak (Cray-1). Everyone on Earth: about 8.2 billion people.
